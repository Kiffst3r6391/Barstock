Three faces for the same well-run bar.

The screens below are the same dashboard, styled three ways. The rail, the four headline figures, the attention list and the trend all stay put. What changes is the character: how the app reads to a committee, to the person on the floor, and to a club that has never seen it before.

Direction A

The Ledger

The club's bar, reported like the club's accounts. Ruled lines, serif figures and nothing that looks like a gadget. It is built to survive a finance committee.

Palette
Chalk Bottle Copper Ink
Type
Source Serif 4 for figures and headings (Georgia in the app) · Public Sans for labels (SF Pro Text in the app) · IBM Plex Mono for dates (Menlo in the app)
Density
High. Four figures on one line, no boxes, one rule under every heading.

Where it wins

  • Four figures a treasurer can check at a glance: stock at cost, sales since the last baseline, active lines and what needs chasing.
  • The same rules and serif figures carry into all 14 reports and the committee PDF, so the pack looks like the app rather than a spreadsheet export.
  • The attention list tells the operational truth — two stale tabs and an overdue sales upload — instead of inventing reorder levels the club does not set.

What it costs

  • Slower to scan on a busy Friday: urgency comes from the attention list and copper rules, not from colour blocks.
  • Dense report tables need width; the shell is comfortable at 1440px, but a small laptop will scroll.
  • Feels formal for a bar manager who wants a quick answer before service.

Direction B

Night Service

For the person running the bar: a dark console that answers "what do I need to fix before we open?" in about two seconds. Big figures, one amber signal, no decoration.

Palette
Graphite Panel Brass Chalk
Type
Archivo for interface text · IBM Plex Mono for every figure, so columns line up
Density
High, but spaced. Tiles carry their own border and sit on a darker ground.

Where it wins

  • Designed for the cellar at six in the evening: low light, a glance, a decision.
  • Status colour has room to read as a signal, so "below par" and "order" stand out immediately.
  • Photographs well on a projector or a laptop at a trade stand, and looks like modern software.

What it costs

  • Dark interfaces split opinion: some managers will ask for a light version on day one.
  • Reports and printed sheets need a light counterpart, so you carry two skins, not one.
  • The contrast budget is tighter; every new status colour has to be tested against graphite.

Direction C

The Green

A light, calm clubhouse look that a manager can pick up without training. Soft cards, a lot of air, and one green that does the work of a logo.

Palette
Stone White Club green Clay
Type
Bricolage Grotesque for headings · Instrument Sans for text and figures
Density
Low. Cards with generous padding, two panels per row, figures given room.

Where it wins

  • The easiest of the three to hand to someone who has never seen the app; hierarchy does the teaching.
  • Looks right on a website, in a proposal and on a phone, which matters when you are selling.
  • Friendly without being a toy, so it still suits a members' club committee.

What it costs

  • Cards and air cost vertical space, so fewer figures fit on a small laptop screen.
  • The soft-card look is common; the club's own accent colour has to carry the identity.
  • Needs discipline: one more card style and it turns into a dashboard of boxes.

What all three share

Every direction uses the same information architecture: the club rail, four headline figures, a short attention list, a second panel and the latest deliveries. Picking a look changes nothing about how the app works, which is why this is a decision you can make on taste alone.

Club identity at first run

Club name, crest initials and one accent colour. Everything else stays quiet, so a new club looks set up rather than skinned.

One set of tokens

Colours, type and spacing already live in ui_theme.py. A theme is a setting, not a rewrite, so you can sell the same build to every club.

Print parity

Reports draw from the same tokens, so the committee PDF looks like the screen the manager showed them.

  1. Demo mode. A sample club with sample figures, a visible "demo" badge, and a reset button. Sales demos never touch a real club's numbers.
  2. First-run setup. Club name, crest, VAT basis, suppliers, measures and an opening stocktake, in that order, with a sensible default at every step.
  3. Committee pack. One PDF a month: sales, gross profit, stock holding, variance and top movers, ready to attach to a board paper.
  4. Invoice import that survives real paperwork. Booker and Tennent's layouts, a price-check review, and a clear list of lines it could not match.
  5. A backup you can hand over. One dated file, restorable by the next manager without you on the phone.
  6. An update path. Signed builds, the version in the window title, and a quiet check for a newer release.

Go with The Ledger

It is the direction chosen for Prestonfield: chalk paper, bottle green and copper, with the club's real figures. It reads as a set of accounts, which is what a treasurer or club secretary wants to sign, and it stays legible on the older laptops and projectors in the club office.

It replaces the current dark copper/navy theme rather than sitting beside it, so every screen, dialog and PDF carries one identity. Night Service and The Green remain useful comparisons, not alternate skins to ship.
